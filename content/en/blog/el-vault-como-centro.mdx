I keep an Obsidian vault with three-hundred-odd notes. At first it was what it is
for almost everyone: a place to leave things so I would not forget them. Now it
is something else, and the change did not come from taking better notes.

## What I actually use it for

Two jobs, and neither is "taking notes".

**It manages my content production.** Every video script lives there before it
is shot, with its structure, its hook and its tags. It is not a loose draft: it
is the channel's unit of work.

**It documents my projects.** Each project has its folder with the business
context, the stack, the modules and — the most valuable part — a results file
with the lessons. When I went back to code I had not looked at in three months,
I did not have to reconstruct why I had decided what I decided. It was written
down.

That second use is the one that has paid off most, and it is the least
glamorous: **writing down why you did something costs ten minutes and saves you
an afternoon six months later.**

## The day it stopped being an archive

The change came when one of my apps started writing into it.

The system that measures my channel exposes a tool that does something very
simple: it finds the video that came out of a given script and returns its
metrics as a block ready to paste into that script's note. The link between
script and video does not live in any database — it is a convention in the
video's own text.

The result is that the script's note **carries how it did**. I do not have to
open the dashboard, find the video, copy numbers and paste them. The loop closes
on its own: I write the script, shoot, publish, and the metrics come back to
where it all started.

It sounds like convenience. It is more than that: when performance lives next
to the text that produced it, **you can ask what kind of script works** instead
of which video worked. And that is a question that does change what you do
tomorrow.

## Why it appreciates

Here is what made me take it seriously.

A vault is not worth what it holds today. It is worth what it is going to hold.
Every note you add does not add up: it **multiplies**, because the value is in
the crossings — this project used that decision, this script worked like that
other one, I already made this mistake somewhere else.

And there is a second, more recent reason. The more I work with agents, the more
it becomes clear that what decides whether an agent helps me or gets in the way
**is not the model: it is how much it knows about me**. An agent with access to
three years of documented decisions, scripts with their results and projects
with their reasons is a different tool from one that starts from zero every
conversation.

Put another way: **every note I write today is context an agent will use two
years from now.** The vault is not time spent, it is an asset that compounds.

## What I changed once I understood that

I write differently since then. Less for tomorrow's me and more for **someone —
or something — that will read this without having been there**.

That means concrete things: putting down the why and not just the what; dating
decisions, because circumstances change; and recording mistakes in the same
detail as successes, since they tend to be more useful.

And one rule that has served me well: if I have explained something twice, it
goes in the vault. The second time you ask yourself the same question is the
signal.

## What is missing

I still carry things by hand. The vault knows nothing yet about my repositories,
or what I have published, or what I am working on this week.

That is the work ahead: **getting more systems to write into it**. Each one I
connect reduces what I have to type and increases what the whole knows. It is
the same idea I chase when I put an MCP server on my applications — which I
wrote about [here](/en/blog/mcp-sobre-tu-propia-app) — seen from the other end:
there I open my apps so they can be queried; here I collect what they return.
